    Drift–Diffusion Decomposition for Likelihood-Based SDE Learning
                  with Adaptive Random Fourier Features
                 Owen Douglas, Aku Kammonen, Anamika Pandey, Raul Tempone
                                                    February 2026

                                                        Abstract
         Learning stochastic dynamics from limited observations is a central challenge in data-driven modelling
     of Itô SDEs. We present a training procedure for estimating drift and diffusion from snapshot triples
     (x0 , x1 , h) under the Gaussian transition density induced by Euler–Maruyama discretisation. Our approach
     exploits the structure of the resulting negative log-likelihood: fixing the diffusion reduces the loss to a least-
     squares regression for the drift increments, and the pointwise likelihood minimisers for the diffusion then
     serve as regression targets for learning the diffusion covariance. This sequential formulation enables efficient
     training using adaptive random Fourier features (ARFF), which combines frequency resampling, random-
     walk updates, and closed-form least-squares solves. Experiments on polynomial systems, an underdamped
     Langevin model, a stochastic SIR mean-field limit, a stochastic wave equation, and a near-singular spectral
     benchmark show that the proposed method attains comparable or better final accuracy than Adam, with
     comparable or faster convergence across all benchmarks considered. In the near-singular benchmark, we
     further observe that Adam itself performs substantially better when applied to the sequential regression
     problems than when optimising the joint negative log-likelihood directly.


1    Introduction
Identifying stochastic dynamics from limited and noisy observations is a central challenge across science and
engineering. Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) provide flexible models for noisy, multiscale systems in
physics [25, 32], finance [1, 13], and biology [27]. Classical estimation methods—such as maximum likelihood,
method of moments, and Bayesian inference [21, 28]—require strong model assumptions and substantial ana-
lytical work, and can become computationally prohibitive in complex or high-dimensional systems. Classical
statistics for discretely observed diffusions provides maximum-likelihood approximations, estimating functions,
Bayesian methods, and nonparametric coefficient estimators [3, 5, 6, 7, 8]. These methods are powerful, but
often require model-specific transition-density approximations, asymptotic regimes, or nontrivial numerical in-
tegration. Our setting emphasises scattered snapshot triples, flexible multivariate covariance regression, and
computational training through adaptive random features.
Recent machine learning approaches aim to relax these limitations by fitting drift and diffusion directly from
data. Neural SDEs [31] and SDE-Net [17] parametrise the coefficients with neural networks, while [5, 6] proposed
likelihood-based training under Euler–Maruyama discretisation. Gaussian-process and related nonparametric
formulations have also been explored [20], and neural SDE variants [31, 17, 5, 6] are promising; however, neural
networks typically rely on gradient-based training (e.g., Adam), which motivates seeking a simpler alternative.
Random Fourier features (RFF) [23, 20] offer a contrasting approach: functions are represented with randomised
trigonometric bases, and training reduces to closed-form least squares. Recent works have further advanced the
theoretical understanding and extensions of RFF: Szabó & Sriperumbudur study derivative approximations via
RFF [29], Li et al. propose quadrature-based Fourier features for scalable Gaussian process regression [19], and
Yao et al. introduce bootstrap error estimation techniques for RFFs [35]. Adaptive variants (ARFF) [14, 15, 12]
iteratively resample and perturb frequencies toward the optimal Fourier spectrum, yielding improved accuracy
and stability. The ARFF approach was first proposed in [14], where the Fourier frequencies are adapted using a
Metropolis-based sampling. This built upon earlier RFF works such as [23, 24, 3, 2], where authors attempted
to identify optimal frequency sampling distributions or feature weights. Later, [15] enhanced this idea by adding
a resampling step, inspired by particle-filtering, which improved the stability of the training with respect to
hyperparameter choices such as proposal step length and acceptance exponents. To our knowledge, ARFF has
not yet been explored for likelihood-based SDE training. The present manuscript substantially revises and
extends our earlier ARFF-for SDE-learning preprint by sharpening the drift–diffusion split, adding near-singular
covariance diagnostics, and expanding the empirical analysis.
This sequential formulation enables training with adaptive random Fourier features (ARFF), whose loops consist
only of resampling, random-walk updates in frequency space, and closed-form least-squares solves, yielding a


                                                             1
simple alternative to gradient-based optimisation for learning SDEs from snapshot data. Importantly, the
benefits of this sequential formulation are not tied to the ARFF training algorithm itself: even when trained
with the same optimiser (Adam), the sequential regression problems can improve training behavior compared
with direct optimisation of the joint likelihood.
The key observation underlying this work is that the Gaussian Euler–Maruyama likelihood identifies the drift
through a conditional mean and the covariance through a conditional second moment. This motivates a se-
quential estimator: first regress scaled increments to estimate the drift, then regress residual outer products to
estimate the covariance. The resulting algorithm avoids differentiating through inverse covariance matrices and
can be trained efficiently with adaptive random Fourier features (ARFF).
The key observation underlying this work is that the Gaussian Euler–Maruyama negative log-likelihood admits
a structural decomposition into two independent regression problems: one for the drift and one for the diffu-
sion covariance. This decoupling is non-trivial, as the original likelihood couples the two through the inverse
covariance, and it enables the use of closed-form least-squares solvers in place of gradient-based optimisation.

Contributions (i) We derive a likelihood-based decomposition of the Gaussian Euler–Maruyama loss, which
reduces SDE training to two tractable regression problems yields a two-stage regression-based training proce-
dure for drift and diffusion. (ii) We show how these regression problems can be solved efficiently with adaptive
random Fourier features (ARFF), yielding a new training procedure (Alg. 2) tailored to SDEs. This approach
avoids backpropagation through nonlinear networks by relying instead on closed-form least-squares solves com-
bined with adaptive frequency sampling. (iii) We provide a complexity analysis and demonstrate that our
method achieves comparable or better final accuracy than Adam, with comparable or faster convergence across
polynomial, Langevin, epidemic, spectrally wide, and SPDE benchmarks. (iv) We demonstrate empirically,
through a near-singular diffusion covariance example, that the split formulation can improve convergence com-
pared with direct optimisation of the joint negative log-likelihood, even when both approaches use the same
optimiser (Adam).

4     Drift and Diffusion Training Algorithm
This section presents how the likelihood formulation in Section 3 reduces to two tractable regression problems:
one for the drift fθ and one for the diffusion covariance Σθ′ . These are first derived at the population level,
i.e. as expectations with respect to the true distribution ρ, and are then approximated by empirical averages
over the dataset D. In practice, we approximate the minimisers of these least-squares problems using neural
networks trained with adaptive random Fourier features (ARFF) (Alg. 1), i.e., linear models in Fourier features
with coefficients obtained via least-squares solves.
In particular, drift learning reduces to a regression of the scaled increments h−1 (x1 − x0 ) onto fθ (x0 ), while
diffusion learning reduces to a regression of the conditional covariance h−1 rr⊤ onto Σθ′ (x0 ).
This section presents the proposed drift and diffusion training procedure. Under the Gaussian Euler–Maruyama
observation model, the drift and diffusion covariance are characterised by the conditional mean of the scaled
increments and the conditional second moment of the residuals, respectively. These identities motivate the
regression surrogates used in Alg. 2.
For a symmetric matrix A, the relation A ≻ 0 means A is (symmetric) positive definite, and A ⪰ 0 means
positive semidefinite. For a vector v and M ≻ 0, we write ∥v∥2M := v ⊤ M v.

4.3    Split vs joint training
Existing SDE learning approaches employ joint gradient-based optimisation of the likelihood (5) [5, 6]. In
contrast, Alg. 2 adopts a split regression-based formulation. This leads to two main fundamental learning
differences: (i) dependence on the inverse covariance, and (ii) enforcement of positive definite covariance.

Dependence on the inverse covariance. In joint likelihood-based training, the loss depends explicitly on
the precision matrix (hΣθ (x))−1 . Consequently, learning requires repeated inversion of the covariance. When
Σθ (x) is close to singular, the precision matrix becomes ill-conditioned, amplifying errors and leading to unstable
behaviour.
In contrast, the split formulation avoids any explicit dependence on the inverse covariance. The covariance
diffusion is learned via regression on empirical covariance targets constructed from residuals, reducing the
problem to a sequence of well-conditioned least-squares-type problems. As a result, the method can remain
stable even when the diffusion is small or near-singular.

Positive definite covariance. Joint training enforces positive definiteness structurally through its parametri-
sation, typically via a Cholesky-type factorisation. This guarantees that the learned covariance is symmetric
positive definite at every point, and therefore always defines a valid diffusion model.
In the split formulation, the empirical covariance targets are inherently positive semidefinite, providing well-
behaved training data. However, positive definiteness is not enforced pointwise in the learned function, and the
resulting covariance field may fail to be positive definite away from the training data.

Implications. These differences lead to distinct stability properties. Joint training guarantees a valid co-
variance but is sensitive to ill-conditioning through the inverse covariance. The split formulation avoids this
instability by construction, whilst not explicitly enforcing positive definiteness of the learned covariance.
These effects are investigated empirically in Section 6.

4.5    Computational complexity
Alg. 2 calls ARFF twice, once for the drift and once for the diffusion. In each call we first perform an initial
regularised least-squares solve and then repeat up to M iterations of resample → random walk in feature space →
rebuild features → solve LS, where the iteration count satisfies Mmin ≤ M ≤ Mmax depending on early stopping.
Constructing (i.e., evaluating or rebuilding) the random Fourier features for N samples in D dimensions to form
the design matrix S costs O(N KD) [23]. Since the random walk changes the frequencies, this step is repeated in
every iteration; below we simply refer to it as “rebuilding the features.” The resampling step requires computing
weights and redrawing K features, costing O(DK) in practice. This is negligible compared to feature evaluation
and least-squares solves, but we mention it here for completeness. The dominant work remains the LS solve.

the conjugate gradient (CG) method avoid the cubic term. The least-squares solve per rebuild is

residual-based targets, yielding a more stable and reliable learning signal. As a result, split training provides
a more robust approach for learning SDEs with near-singular covariance structure. Among the split methods,
Alg. 2 consistently achieved the fastest convergence.
Although the split formulation does not enforce positive definiteness of the learned covariance, no violations
were observed in our experimentation.

7    Summary and Discussion
This work introduces a training procedure (Alg. 2) for likelihood-based learning of Itô SDEs from snapshot
data. The key contribution is a derivation showing that the Gaussian negative log-likelihood induced by Euler–
Maruyama discretisation decomposes into two tractable regression problems: one for the drift and one for the
diffusion covariance. This enables the use of adaptive random Fourier features (ARFF) in place of gradient-based
training.
Across polynomial, Langevin, epidemic, stochastic PDE, and wide-spectrum benchmarks, Alg. 2 attains loss
values and coefficient errors comparable to or better than Adam-trained baselines (Fourier-feature, shallow tanh,
and deep tanh), often in reduced training time.
The near-singular diffusion benchmark exposes a structural weakness of joint likelihood-based training. The
likelihood depends on the inverse covariance (hΣθ′ (x0 ))−1 , so when the diffusion becomes near-singular, residual
errors are strongly amplified. This distorts the optimisation problem and leads to unstable behaviour and
inaccurate recovery of the drift and diffusion.
By contrast, the split formulation avoids this amplification by learning the diffusion from residual-based covari-
ance targets. As a result, the split methods, for both Adam and ARFF optimisation, recover substantially more
accurate drift and diffusion. Of the split methods, the ARFF implementation achieved the fastest convergence.

Limitations and future work Our benchmarks are primarily low- to moderate-dimensional. Extending the
approach to higher-dimensional systems, more complex diffusion structures, and larger datasets is an important
next step.


                                                        20
In contrast to joint training, the split formulation does not explicitly enforce positive definiteness of the learned
covariance. The residual outer-product targets are positive semidefinite but generally rank deficient. Their
conditional mean may be positive definite under a nondegeneracy condition on the residual distribution. Since
the split ARFF implementation regresses covariance entries componentwise, the learned covariance field is not
guaranteed to be positive definite unless an SPD projection or structured parametrization is added.
In practice, however, the empirical covariance targets are positive definite, and no violations were observed in
the learned models. Incorporating structured parametrisations to guarantee positive definiteness could further
improve robustness.
A further finite-sample consideration is that the same training data are used to estimate the drift and to
construct the residual covariance targets. As discussed in Section 4.2, this means that the plug-in targets are
not guaranteed to be conditionally unbiased at finite sample size. Cross-fitting, in which each residual target
is constructed using a drift estimate trained without that observation, provides a natural way to remove this
same-sample dependence and is left for future work.
Other possible directions include hybrid approaches in which ARFF provides an efficient initialisation followed by
gradient-based refinement, as well as comparisons with alternative baselines such as Gaussian process regression
or kernel score matching.
A further extension is to move beyond the Euler–Maruyama likelihood. While EM yields a tractable Gaussian
objective, its discretisation introduces a lower-order approximation of the diffusion. Incorporating higher-order
schemes or alternative likelihood formulations may enable more accurate recovery of diffusion coefficients.
In preliminary experiments, we also tested repeatedly alternating between drift and diffusion training. This did
not lead to further reductions in validation error beyond the single drift–diffusion pass used in Alg. 2, and in
some cases degraded performance; we therefore focus on the simpler single-pass procedure.
In summary, the main contribution is the likelihood-based drift–diffusion decomposition, which enables a stable
and efficient alternative to joint gradient-based optimisation. The resulting method provides a simple and
effective framework for data-driven SDE identification, with particular advantages in regimes where the diffusion
is ill-conditioned.
