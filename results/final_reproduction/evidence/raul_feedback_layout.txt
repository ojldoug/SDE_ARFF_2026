1    How to read this verified feedback
Severity labels follow the A–D scale requested in the manuscript-review workflow.

• blocker = severity A: correctness or validity issue; must be fixed before an arXiv update or
  journal submission.

• major = severity B: important rigor, clarity, positioning, numerical, or reproducibility issue.

• minor = severity C: exposition or structural improvement.

• suggestion = severity D: presentation or optional style improvement.

• strength: brief note about what is already working.

Every substantive point is anchored to ARFFSDELearning.pdf by page, section, equation, table,
figure, or algorithm when available. The audit file is treated as the verification layer: where the
pre-audit feedback and the audit differed, I adopted the audit-supported correction.


2    Audit-integrated revision note
The attached audit confirmed the main mathematical blockers in the pre-audit feedback and
sharpened several of them. The verified version below now makes five changes relative to the
first feedback file. First, it states the drift result as a weighted conditional-moment identity and
distinguishes it from the ordinary ridge surrogate used in the algorithm. Second, it corrects the
diffusion proposition to strict convexity in the precision variable, not in the covariance variable,
and adds the scalar counterexample. Third, it treats residual covariance targets as out-of-fold or
fixed-drift targets, not automatically unbiased same-sample targets. Fourth, it strengthens the
covariance-validity recommendation: raw residual outer products are positive semidefinite and rank
deficient, and the learned covariance must be projected or parametrized to be SPD before likelihood
evaluation or simulation. Fifth, it qualifies the error decomposition and the experiment narrative,
especially for fine-step SDE snapshots, the event-level SSA/SIR experiment, and the near-singular
benchmark.

3     Executive summary
3.1   Summary of contribution as understood
The paper proposes a likelihood-motivated method for learning Itô SDE coefficients from snapshot
triples (x0 , x1 , h). The central computational idea is to avoid direct joint optimization of the
Gaussian Euler–Maruyama negative log-likelihood by using conditional-moment identities: first
estimate the drift from scaled increments, then estimate the covariance from residual outer products.
Both stages are trained with adaptive random Fourier features (ARFF), so the main numerical work
is repeated ridge-type least-squares solution with adaptive frequency resampling and random-walk
updates.

3.2   Top-line assessment
• Core idea. The method is useful and potentially publishable: a sequential conditional-moment
  estimator is a natural way to reduce the ill-conditioning created by inverse covariance matrices
  in joint Gaussian NLL training.

• Main empirical strength. Experiment 8 is compelling. It shows that low joint NLL can
  coexist with poor coefficient recovery in a near-singular covariance regime, while split training
  recovers drift and covariance more faithfully.

• Current readiness. I do not recommend updating arXiv or submitting to SIMODS until the
  mathematical statements in §4 are rewritten. The current draft overstates the exactness of the
  decomposition.

• Most important mathematical correction. The Gaussian NLL gives unrestricted population
  conditional-moment identities. It does not decompose into two independent finite-sample ordinary
  least-squares problems in the restricted ARFF class.

• Second correction. The diffusion objective is strictly convex in the precision P = a−1 , not in
  the covariance a or Σ.

• Third correction. Individual empirical covariance targets h−1 rr⊤ are PSD and rank at most
  one; they are not positive definite for D > 1.

• Implementation risk. The paper must define how the learned covariance is made SPD before
  computing log-determinants, inverses, validation NLL, and learned trajectories.

• Venue fit. SIMODS is a plausible target after revision. The journal scope is aligned with
  mathematical, statistical, and computational methods for data science, and its referee instructions
  explicitly emphasize novelty, correctness, significance, technical soundness, reproducibility, and
  state-of-the-art comparisons [1, 2].
